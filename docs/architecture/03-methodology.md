# 03 — Theoretical Foundations

Every architectural decision in this platform is grounded in published methodology. This document collects the key justifications with direct citations from the primary sources.

---

## 1. Why Out-of-Sample Evaluation at Every Level

The signal engine, the strategy review, and the backtest layer all evaluate on data that was never used for fitting. This is not a convention — it is a statistical necessity.

### The problem with in-sample results

> *"One of the purposes of ML is to learn the general structure of the data, so that we can produce predictions on future, unseen features. When we test an ML algorithm on the same dataset as was used for training, not surprisingly, we achieve spectacular results. When ML algorithms are misused that way, they are no different from file lossy-compression algorithms: They can summarize the data with extreme fidelity, yet with zero forecasting power."*
> — López de Prado (2018), Ch.7, p.103

### The danger of repeated backtesting

> *"Even if your backtest is flawless, it is probably wrong. Why? Because only an expert can produce a flawless backtest. Becoming an expert means that you have run tens of thousands of backtests over the years. In conclusion, this is not the first backtest you produce, so we need to account for the possibility [of selection bias on multiple tests]."*
> — López de Prado (2018), Ch.11, p.152–153

### How we apply this

| Layer | What is evaluated OOS | How |
|-------|----------------------|-----|
| Signal engine (Layer B) | Indicator predictive power | Rolling walk-forward windows; returns computed on data the indicator never saw during parameter selection |
| Signal engine (Layer C) | Robustness | Multi-component score across OOS windows, not IS metrics |
| Backtest WFO | Strategy parameter transfer | Each IS window's winner tested on a non-overlapping OOS window |
| Backtest test period | Final validation | Held-out data never seen by any part of the optimization process |

## 2. Why Walk-Forward Analysis

Walk-Forward Analysis (WFA) is the core evaluation methodology for the backtest layer, following Pardo (2008) throughout.

### The case for WFA

> *"The performance of a trading strategy on data which were never part of the optimization process is a far more reliable measure than performance based solely on in-sample simulation."*
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.11, p.237

> *"Walk-Forward Analysis provides answers to four essential questions that are necessary to begin and to continue trading a strategy with intelligence and confidence:*
> *1. Is the trading strategy robust? Will it make money in real-time trading?*
> *2. What rate of return will the trading strategy produce in real-time trading?*
> *3. How will the typical changes in market behavior such as trend, volatility and liquidity affect trading performance?*
> *4. What are the best parameters to use in real-time trading to produce maximum profit with minimum risk?"*
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.11, p.237

### WFA as the cure for overfitting

> *"I have always viewed Walk-Forward Analysis as an idiot-proof way of optimizing a trading strategy. Extensive experience has not proven this wrong."*
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.11, p.240

> *"One of the greatest advantages of Walk-Forward Analysis is that it is very difficult to abuse and consequently, whereas it is possible, it is very difficult (even if not impossible) for the strategist using WFA — with his eyes open — to overfit a trading strategy."*
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.11, p.240

### Walk-Forward Efficiency (WFE)

> *"A trading strategy is likely overfit if it has a low Walk-Forward Efficiency; in other words, if the rate of return of out-of-sample trading is decidedly lower than that of in-sample trading."*
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.11, p.239

> *"Research has clearly demonstrated that robust trading strategies have WFEs greater than 50 or 60 percent and in the case of extremely robust strategies, even higher. In fact, there is no reason why a trading strategy cannot exceed in-sample performance in out-of-sample testing as well as in real-time trading."*
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.11, p.239

Our acceptance threshold: **WFE ≥ 50%**. Strategies below this are flagged as not viable with diagnostic data.

## 3. Why PROM as Objective Function

The backtest WFO engine uses Pessimistic Return on Margin (PROM) as its objective function, not Sharpe ratio, not net profit.

### The problem with net profit

> *"Research has shown that highest net profit alone is not an adequate measure of the overall trading performance and robustness of a trading model. For example, 60 percent of the highest net profit could be generated by a strategy with one large and likely unrepeatable trade, such as a short trade during the October 1987 stock market crash."*
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.9, p.202

### What a good objective function selects

> *"The most robust and stable trading strategy may or may not be the most profitable. The most stable trading strategy will be one that exhibits [these] characteristics:"*
>
> 1. A relatively even distribution of trades
> 2. A relatively even distribution of trading profit
> 3. Relative balance between long and short profit
> 4. A large group of contiguous, profitable strategy parameters in the optimization
> 5. Acceptable trading performance in a wide range of markets
> 6. Acceptable risk
> 7. Relatively stable winning and losing runs
> 8. A large and statistically valid number of trades
> 9. A positive performance trajectory
>
> — Pardo (2008), Ch.9, p.203

PROM naturally addresses points 6, 7, and 8: its square-root adjustment penalizes small trade samples, and its construction incorporates both win rate and risk (average loss). See `backtest-layer/03-prom-and-objective-function.md` for the full formula.

## 4. Why Ensemble Over Selection

The signal engine combines surviving representatives via reliability-weighted averaging rather than picking "the best" variant.

### The statistical argument

Selecting a single best performer from multiple candidates is itself a form of overfitting — the selected variant may be best due to noise, not signal. Ensemble methods reduce variance by averaging across multiple models.

> *"Backtesting is not a research tool. Feature importance is."*
> — López de Prado (2018), Ch.8, Snippet 8.1

De Prado dedicates Ch.6 to ensemble methods (bagging, random forests, boosting), establishing that combining weak learners produces stronger predictors than selecting a single best model. Our signal engine applies this principle: each surviving representative is a "weak learner" whose reliability is known from OOS evaluation, and the ensemble is a reliability-weighted average.

### How we apply this

- Layer E reduces redundancy (correlated variants eliminated)
- Layer F computes current signals for each surviving representative
- Layer G combines via `score = Σ(signal_i × weight_i) / Σ(weight_i)` where weights are reliability scores
- The ensemble score is more stable than any individual variant's score

## 5. Why 4 Indicator Families Only

The platform uses exactly four indicator families: SMA (trend), RSI (oscillation), MACD (momentum), OBV (volume). This is a deliberate constraint.

### The classification principle

Elder (1993) established that technical indicators fall into three categories — trend-following, oscillator, and volume — and that indicators within the same category are largely redundant while indicators from different categories capture orthogonal market dimensions.

Our four families map to Elder's three categories plus a momentum distinction:

| Family | Elder Category | What it captures |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| SMA | Trend-following | Price position relative to its trend |
| MACD | Trend-following (acceleration) | Rate of change of the trend |
| RSI | Oscillator | Mean-reversion extremes |
| OBV | Volume | Volume-price confirmation |

### Parsimony over complexity

Each family generates 30 candidates per horizon = 120 total variants per (symbol, horizon). This is a manageable multiple-testing burden.

Adding more families (Stochastic, CCI, Bollinger, etc.) would increase the variant count and multiply the risk of false discoveries — the exact problem that de Prado (2018) Ch.8 warns against. Additional families are planned as future expansions within existing categories, not new categories.

### Academic grounding

- SMA: Murphy (1999), Ch.9 — the simplest and most widely used trend indicator
- RSI: Wilder (1978), Ch.1 — relative strength as mean-reversion signal
- MACD: Appel (2005), Ch.3 — EMA convergence/divergence as momentum
- OBV: Granville (1963) — cumulative volume as price confirmation

Each indicator has decades of practitioner literature and a clear economic interpretation. No black-box features.

## 6. Why Continuous Scores

The signal engine pipeline (Layers A–G) uses discrete signals (+1, -1, 0) for OOS evaluation. But the indicator explorer and strategy entry/exit rules use **continuous scores**.

### The information argument

A price 0.5 ATR above SMA and a price 4.0 ATR above SMA both produce +1 in the discrete signal framework. But they carry very different information:

- 0.5 ATR: mild trend confirmation, close to neutral
- 4.0 ATR: strong trend or potential overextension

Continuous scores preserve this distance-from-neutral information, enabling:
- Graduated entry at multiple conviction levels
- Dynamic position sizing based on signal strength
- Visual exploration of indicator behavior (Indicateurs tab)

### ATR normalization

Two of four scoring formulas use ATR (Average True Range) as the denominator:

```
SMA score  = (close - SMA) / ATR
MACD score = MACD_histogram / ATR
```

This makes scores dimensionless and comparable across stocks with different price levels and volatility regimes. A score of 2.0 means "2 ATR above the indicator" regardless of whether the stock trades at 50 or 500.

> ATR was introduced by Wilder (1978) specifically as a volatility normalizer for technical indicators. It remains the standard approach (Murphy 1999, Elder 1993).

RSI is already self-normalized [0, 100] by construction. OBV uses self-normalization `(OBV - OBV_EMA) / OBV_EMA` because it is cumulative volume, not price.

## 7. Why the Degrees-of-Freedom Constraint

The backtest IS window must satisfy:

```
IS >= 10 × max_lookback    (Pardo Ch.6 p.163)
```

This ensures that the IS window contains at least 10 times as many observations as the longest lookback parameter, keeping degrees of freedom above 90%. With fewer observations, the optimizer finds patterns in noise rather than signal.

This is why the backtest IS window is always **larger** than the signal engine's train window (252/504/756 bars). The signal engine windows are for computing returns over fixed evaluation periods. The backtest IS window must be large enough for statistically valid optimization.
