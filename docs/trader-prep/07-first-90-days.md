# First 90 Days on the Desk — Onboarding & the Social Playbook

**Context (2026-07-19):** verbal yes from the manager after the 6-month
end-of-studies internship (where this app was built); awaiting HR. This file
adapts the program from interview prep to day-one readiness. `06-interview-prep.md`
is demoted to backup (re-read it only if HR asks for a formal interview round).

The honest self-assessment behind this file: the technical gap is covered by
`01`–`05`; the bigger first-year risk is the social/culture gap (quiet by
nature, floor culture is sociable and French-speaking, ~6 conversations in 6
months of internship). That gap is trainable, and it is trained the same way as
mental math: small daily reps, not personality change.

## Principle: you don't need to become extroverted

Trading floors have plenty of quiet, precise people — usually the ones trusted
with risk. Juniors are judged on: reliable, curious, present, owns mistakes
instantly. None of those require charisma. What is NOT survivable is being
*invisible* — so the playbook below converts "be sociable" (vague, terrifying)
into small concrete actions with low activation energy and a daily cap.

## The social playbook (daily reps, like the drills)

- **The blotter is your conversation engine.** Floor small talk is the market.
  Every morning you arrive knowing what moved and why — that's one ready-made
  opener per day: "vous avez vu le CPI hier soir ?" / "le 2s10s a bougé de
  15bp, c'est le discours de la Fed ?" One market comment to one person per
  day. That's the whole quota at first.
- **Arrive early.** The floor at 7:45 is quiet and 1-on-1; the same
  conversation that's impossible across a loud row at 11am is easy over coffee
  at 7:50. Early birds also happen to be the seniors worth learning from.
- **One question a day, aimed.** Juniors asking specific questions is expected
  and welcomed — it's how desks teach. Rules: specific ("pourquoi on couvre ce
  eurobond avec le future et pas le cash ?"), timed (never mid-execution; catch
  people after the close or at coffee), and *written down* with the answer.
  Your questions log becomes your onboarding curriculum.
- **Names first.** Week 1 goal is not friendships — it's a seat map: name,
  role, what they cover, for everyone within earshot. Greet by name every
  morning ("bonjour Karim"). A daily bonjour to 10 people does more over 90
  days than any single impressive conversation.
- **One lunch/coffee per week.** Ask one person, not a group ("je peux me
  joindre ?" also counts). Prepare two questions about *their* market — people
  are easy to talk to about the thing they trade all day. Weekly, not daily:
  sustainable beats heroic.
- **The app is your social capital — spend it.** People already know you as
  "l'stagiaire qui a construit la plateforme." When someone shows interest,
  offer a 10-minute walkthrough of one screen they'd care about. Teaching is
  the easiest social mode for an introvert: structured, you're the expert, and
  it builds the exact relationships you need.
- **Own errors in under a minute.** The floor's cardinal rule: when you fat-
  finger, mis-book, or misunderstand — say it immediately, loudly enough, with
  the fix. "Je me suis trompé sur X, voilà la correction" builds more trust
  than a month of flawless silence. Never let anyone discover your error
  before you've announced it.
- **Debrief rep (weekly, in the Saturday review):** one social interaction that
  went fine (evidence against the impostor story), one that was awkward and
  what you'd say next time. Same format as grading blotter calls — the point
  is reps and review, not perfection.

## French for the floor (daily, 15 min)

The vocabulary gap is financial-French fluency at speaking speed, not French
itself. Daily rep: one article or video from **Les Échos / L'Économiste /
BFM Business / Boursorama**, read one paragraph *aloud*, and keep a running
glossary (obligation, échéance, courbe des taux, adjudication, écart de
crédit / spread, couverture, contrepartie, carnet d'ordres, prise ferme…).
Your app being French-labelled means you already have much of this — the rep
is converting reading vocabulary into speaking vocabulary. Do the daily blotter
"what next" line in French twice a week.

## Weeks −N to 0 (waiting for HR)

1. Follow up with the manager once, ~2–3 weeks after the verbal yes: short,
   warm, zero anxiety. ("Je me permets de revenir vers vous suite à notre
   échange… n'hésitez pas si les RH ont besoin d'éléments de ma part.")
2. Run the program as designed: blotter + drills daily, syllabus, Build 1 then
   Build 2. Everything transfers directly to the seat.
3. Reconstruct the internship seat map from memory: the ~6 people you talked
   to, plus everyone you can name — role, desk, what you know about them.
   These are your day-one anchors.

## Months 1–3 on the seat (what "good" looks like)

- **Month 1 — absorb and be reliable.** Learn the systems (booking, risk,
  P&L), the desk's book, conventions of the instruments actually traded.
  Morning routine: blotter *before* arriving → one usable line for the
  morning meeting if asked. Deliver every small task fast and perfectly;
  speed-to-trust on menial tasks is how juniors earn interesting ones.
- **Month 2 — be useful in your lane.** Find the desk's recurring manual pain
  (a spreadsheet, a daily recap, a data pull) and quietly automate or improve
  one of them — you have a proven edge there. Rule: ask before building,
  deliver small, never make the desk depend on something only you understand.
- **Month 3 — have views.** Volunteer one market observation per week from
  your blotter in the morning meeting or to your manager. Wrong-but-reasoned
  is fine; that's what juniors are for. Ask for explicit feedback at the
  90-day mark: "qu'est-ce que je devrais faire différemment ?"
- Throughout: keep the questions log, the error-ownership rule, and the
  Saturday review running. The program's daily habits don't stop at hiring —
  they *are* the job.

## Guardrails for the impostor voice

Facts on file: the manager observed six months of your work and said yes; the
desk engaged with your platform seriously enough to redirect its roadmap; the
awkwardness was fully visible the entire time and did not change the outcome.
The voice saying "he was just being nice" has to explain why he invoked HR —
polite rejections don't create follow-up obligations. If HR truly never calls,
the cost of having prepared is zero: everything here transfers to the next
seat. Prepare as if it's real, because the evidence says it is.
